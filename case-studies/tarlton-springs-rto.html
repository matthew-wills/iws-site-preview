Aviation case study

Tarlton Springs: a rejected take-off and runway overrun

A regional turboprop rejects its take-off above V1 at night after two kangaroos cross the runway, strikes them, and leaves the end of the runway. This page follows the investigation from the first notification to the documents it produced: the evidence it gathered, the map it tested, what it established, and what IWS generated from that one record.

Everything in this case study is fictional: the operator, the aerodrome, the aircraft, the people and every record. The screenshots are real screens from the investigation file, and the documents are published exactly as the application produced them, faults and all.

1 What happened

A rejected take-off at night, and an aircraft off the end of the runway

This is what was known on the evening of 18/06/2026 and the morning after, before anybody had read a recorder or opened a manual.

SB512 was the last scheduled sector of the day out of Tarlton Springs, a certified regional aerodrome in the remote far west, about 380 nm inland of the operator's home base at Port Carrick. The crew had picked the evening rotation up that morning from a crew who reported unfit, and they left on schedule. The aerodrome was in full darkness, with no town lights anywhere around it. It has no control tower: crews make their own broadcasts on the common traffic advisory frequency (CTAF) and switch the runway lights on themselves through the pilot-activated lighting.

The take-off roll on runway 09 was normal to V1. Two seconds after the V1 call the first officer saw two large kangaroos already on the sealed surface ahead, crossing from the northern side, and the captain rejected the take-off at 118 kt. About a second later the aircraft struck both animals. The No. 2 propeller was damaged and its reverse did not respond for the rest of the stop. The aircraft left the end of runway 09 at about 21 kt and came to rest 62 m into the 90 m runway end safety area.

There was no fire and no evacuation. The 44 passengers walked off by the forward airstair a few minutes later. One cabin crew member later reported bruising, and the aircraft was substantially damaged.

The captain's own report, submitted the next morning under the company's just culture scheme, put two things on the record before anyone asked. He had stopped above V1, and everything he had ever been trained on for a stop or go decision had been an engine problem below V1. And the kangaroos at Tarlton Springs were no surprise to anyone who went there.

2 How it was investigated

The evidence first, then every hypothesis on the map, each one tested

Nine source documents, two interviews and a timeline went into the one record. The recorders came off the aircraft the same day, so the flight data settled the speeds before anyone argued about them. Both flight crew were interviewed once the records were in hand, so the questions could be about what the records did not say.

What each source settled

  • Occurrence notification. The first account: the damage, the injuries, the risk rating and the actions taken that night. It opened the file and seeded the first timeline events.
  • Flight data summary. V1 at 112 kt, the reject at 118 kt, the strike a second later, and a deceleration short of the scheduled accelerate-stop performance. Both engines were making power right up to the reject, which closed the engine failure hypothesis.
  • CTAF recording and CVR extract. The crew's broadcasts and the words in the cockpit across the roll, the reject and the stop. It is the evidence behind the reject decision, and behind the fact that no runway inspection was ever asked for.
  • Weather, light and moon. The roll was five minutes after the end of civil twilight, with no moon. Every source gives a headwind, which excluded the tailwind hypothesis.
  • Air safety report, pilot in command. The origin of two lines of enquiry, the training gap behind the reject and the aerodrome's wildlife history. It is protected, so it informs the investigation without being quoted into anything distributed.
  • Operator procedures and policy extracts. The aerodrome standard, the aerodrome manual, the operations manual's wildlife and rejected take-off sections, the route risk assessment and the change record. The external, organisational and risk control lanes rest on these.
  • Maintenance, training and aerodrome records. The deferred fence, the training gap and the 41 recorded sightings come from here, with the crew's duty records and the evening rotation's roster pattern.
  • The two interview transcripts. The captain's account turned the training question from a complaint into a factor that could be tested. The first officer was pilot flying and saw the animals, so her account is the evidence on detection and on what the runway lighting looked like from the seat.

All nine documents are in the source pack, so the analysis can be read back against the same material the investigator had.

The map, at three points in the work

The investigator began with the critical event, worked backwards to identify nineteen possible events and conditions, then tested each against the available evidence. Two hypotheses — an earlier No. 2 engine power loss and a tailwind during the take-off roll — were subsequently excluded.

One object, from the map to its table

Each AcciMap object carries its own evidence and assessment. As the investigator tests an event or condition, the reasoning is recorded against that object and carried directly into the Evidence and Argument Tables.

Floor escape path lighting checked only every 24 months, not pre-flight is the only object on this map that needed all three tests: it existed, it did not influence the overrun, and it is still important enough to raise. Here it is in the three places it lives.

  1. 1On the map

    Selected on the finished map, in the Risk Controls lane. It is orange, an other risk factor, and it carries no arrows, because it played no part in the overrun. Its corner dot is amber: the investigator left it open while engineering finds out whether the failed part is common across the fleet.

  2. 2Its assessment form

    Existence passed at 100 per cent: the test's coverage and the programme interval are documentary, and the defect on VH-SBQ was found on a functional check and traced to a failed component. Influence failed at 10 per cent. Importance passed at 90 per cent.

  3. 3Its table

    Page 11 of the Evidence and Argument Tables: the same three tests, each with its argument for, its argument against and its reasoning, generated from the form. Nothing is typed twice.

The pre-flight lighting test on this type covers the ceiling emergency lights and the exit signs, not the floor proximity escape path segments, which are checked only at the maintenance programme's 24-month functional test. On VH-SBQ that gap held a live defect. A functional check on 24/06/2026 found two of the six floor segments aft of row 16 on the left side dark on battery power, traced to a failed common ballast. The last dedicated test had been 11 months before the occurrence, so the defect could have stayed undetected for up to 13 more months.

It did not influence this occurrence: no evacuation was commanded, the cabin lights were on throughout, and the disembarkation took 4 minutes 8 seconds without incident. It is still a finding. Change one thing, such as a fire indication, smoke or a night evacuation over the wing, and two dark segments in the aft cabin are the difference between an orderly exit and a blocked one.

3 What it established

Twelve contributing safety factors, in converging chains

Twenty objects and fifteen arrows: twelve contributing safety factors, two other risk factors, two excluded hypotheses and one positive event, with one factor whose Influence is unsettled and one object left open on Existence.

What the map says

The chains converge on the animals on the runway and on the reject above V1. The wildlife chain runs from the standard's threshold through the failed fence to the animals, joined by the inspection criterion through the inspection nobody requested, and by the logged kangaroo peak. Tarlton Springs was never on the list of aerodromes where an inspection after last light is required, because no company aircraft had reported a wildlife strike there in the preceding 24 months. A change-management chain runs from the evening service's approval to the roll in darkness, and the darkness and the missing obstruction training both lead to the reject.

The overrun is reached by two routes: the strike through the deceleration shortfall, and the late reject directly, because a late reject shortens the runway left rather than lowering the deceleration. A green arrow also runs from the positive event, maximum braking within 2 seconds of the reject decision, because that changed the outcome as well. The two orange objects, the evening rotation's roster and the escape path lighting, carry no arrows: they failed Influence, and they stay on the map because they are findings in their own right.

What stays open

One arrow is grey and dashed: the claim that four northern edge lights above nominal intensity delayed the crew's detection of the animals, and so the reject. A photometric check seven days later measured them at 118 to 131 per cent of nominal in the section the animals crossed, at a fixed pilot-activated setting nobody had changed, so Existence passed at 80 per cent. The captain described a corridor of light with everything outside it black. The first officer, who saw the animals, called the lighting normal and saw them clearly once they were on the seal. That shows a deviation. It does not show an effect on detection, so Influence was argued and left inconclusive at 45 per cent, and the report has to say the question is open.

Crew fatigue carries no arrow either. Its Existence was argued and left inconclusive at 50 per cent, so there is nothing established to draw an arrow from, and it stays on the map as tested and set aside.

What the colours and shapes mean
  • Blue with a solid border is a contributing safety factor: it existed and it influenced the occurrence.
  • Orange is an other risk factor: it existed, it did not influence this occurrence, and it is still worth raising.
  • Struck through and dashed is an excluded hypothesis, with the reason on the record.
  • Green and rounded is a positive event, something that limited the outcome.
  • Red and rounded is the critical event, the occurrence itself.
  • White and dashed is not yet tested, or a test left inconclusive.
  • A green arrow is a proven causal path.
  • A grey dashed arrow is a causal path still under testing.
  • A green dot means the object's investigation is complete; amber means it is still being worked.

How the reports were drafted

Each report was drafted section by section from the record, then audited against it. What the audit flagged went to a repair pass, and a repaired section was landed by hand, never on its own. These drafts came from a Claude-based pipeline run through the suite's own sealed command-line transport, standing in for the private endpoint an organisation would use. With AI access off, the report is assembled from the record instead.
