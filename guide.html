Using this guide

What this guide covers, how it is organised, and where the first-run walkthrough and the method itself are explained.

#What this guide covers

The Investigation Workflow Suite maps a safety occurrence as an AcciMap. Every suspected event or condition becomes an object on the map, is tested against evidence for Existence, Influence and Importance, and is connected to its consequences with causal arrows.

A finished map reads in two directions at once. Left to right is time: the sequence that led to the occurrence. Bottom to top is organisational depth: from what individuals did, up through the conditions and controls that shaped their actions, to the organisational influences behind those controls. The causal chains converge on the critical event at the bottom right.

The discipline of the method is that nothing is a finding until it has passed its tests. Objects carry their test state visibly, so anyone reading the map can distinguish a proven contributing factor from an untested idea at a glance.

This User Guide is the reference you come back to. It is organised the way the app is: one section for each tab of an investigation, in the order the tabs run, then Settings, exporting, files, the licence, installing and the keyboard and mouse controls. Each control is described where you meet it, and each part of the method is explained where it operates in the app. Every subsection has its own link, and the filter above the contents finds any word in the guide, headings and text alike.

The pictures are of the worked example investigation that ships with the app, taken by the application itself rather than drawn, so what is in them is what you will see. Click one to enlarge it.

#New to IWS: Getting started

New to the app? Getting started takes one investigation from the start screen to an exported document, in the order the work is done, and is the place to begin. This guide assumes you have been through it, or are looking something up. The reasoning behind the method itself is on the Methodology page.

#The tabs of an investigation

An open investigation shows a row of tabs, each one workspace of the suite: Dashboard, Log, Evidence, Timeline, AcciMap, Record and Draft report. Everything they hold travels in the one .sfm file. Drag the tabs into whatever order suits how you work; the order is remembered on this machine.

#The Help window

Help opens from the header, or with F1. Getting started is the first-run walkthrough, and User Guide is this guide. Three references follow, which the app draws live from its own rules: Object key, the colour and shape of every object state with the path that leads to it; Decision tree, the order the Existence, Influence and Importance questions are asked in; and Canvas layers, what sits on the map and in what order. About names the edition and the licence terms. Close shuts the window, and so does Escape.

#Names used in this guide

The map is the AcciMap, the name its tab carries. Its three tests are Existence, Influence and Importance, always in that order; the short form E/I/I appears only where it names something in the app, such as the Decision tree. The record is everything you have entered: the objects and their tests, the evidence, the timeline, the interviews and the safety actions. AI access has three tiers (No AI, Chat and Endpoint), and a licence has levels (Free, Professional and Organisation), so "tier" always means AI access and "level" always means the licence.

1Dashboard

The investigation at a glance: ten tiles, the checklist, one to-do list and the distribution list.

Where the investigation stands, on the tab you open first. A band of tiles across the top counts what has been gathered and what has been settled: objects on the map and how many are finalised, evidence items, interviews, timeline events, outstanding to-dos, checklist questions answered, report sections drafted and finalised in the style you have chosen, and the errors and warnings the record checks are currently reporting. Every figure is read live from the file, so none of it has to be kept up to date by hand. Click a tile to go to the tab where that work is done. Under the tiles sit the checklist, the to-do list and the distribution list.

#The tiles, in order

Ten tiles run left to right. Objects counts how many objects on the map are finalised out of the total and jumps to the map tab. Evidence counts finalised evidence items out of the total and jumps to Evidence. Interviews counts finalised interviews out of the total and also jumps to Evidence. Timeline counts the number of timeline events and jumps to Timeline. To-dos counts how many are still outstanding; clicking it does nothing, since the list is already on this tab. Checklist counts questions answered out of the total, for the same reason. Drafted counts report sections drafted out of the chosen style's total and jumps to Draft report, and Finalised does the same for sections finalised. Errors and Warnings count the record checks currently sitting at each severity, and both jump to Record.

#The checklist

The checklist is a sequential question flow for the whole investigation, answered Yes, No, or N/A (click an answer again to clear it). Guidance notes sit under the questions where a step needs prompting, including an evidence-gathering list (CCTV, recorded data, audio, records of recent change, impairment and fatigue assessment and so on) to help you think of sources before they are overwritten or lost. It seeds from the template in Settings > Templates when the investigation is created, and from then on belongs to the investigation.

The panel is titled Checklist, with a count reading "{n} of {n} addressed" beside it. + Section adds a new section and asks for its title. Each section carries a chevron that collapses it to its header (clicking the title does the same), so completed sections tuck away, a count of how many of its items are answered, and a × that deletes the whole section. Within a section, each item offers Yes, No and N/A as its answer and a × that removes the item. A New item… (Enter to add) box at the foot of a section, beside its own + Add item button, raises another question in it.

#The to-do list

The to-do list is the other half of the same idea. The checklist asks what an investigation of this kind should cover and is the same every time; a to-do is whatever this one needs next, in your own words, and no template could anticipate most of it: ring someone back on a number, chase the CCTV before it is overwritten, ask the roster office for the prior fortnight. It is the one running list of what still needs doing, fed from the checklist, from objects on the map and from anything you type here. The list is yours as a working tool; nothing on it reaches a report.

The panel is titled To do, with a count reading either "nothing yet" or "{n} of {n} left". Type into Add something to do… and click Add to raise one. Items stay in the order you added them. Each row carries its own tick, its text and a × that drops it. Ticking an item writes a line to the Log as a record of what was done and moves the item into a collapsed Completed section beneath the list, which you can expand to check or untick; unticking moves it straight back. Dropping an item with the cross removes it without logging anything, because it was never done.

A to-do can also be raised from inside an object's form on the AcciMap, just under its notepad, for whatever you need to chase up before you can finish that object. It is the same list, not a separate one: it shows here too, with a from: <object> line you can click to jump straight to that object on the AcciMap, which is brought into view and flashed so you can see exactly which one it is (double-click the line to open its form instead). What still needs doing is visible whether you are working the to-do list or the map, and it is easy to hand a job to another investigator.

#The distribution list

Distribution is who this investigation's outputs go to, as a table with a Title column and a Name column. Each row carries Move up, Move down and Remove this row, and + Row adds another beneath them. The list is appended automatically to the foot of every exported report, so it is never left off by hand. Copy as table puts the same rows on the clipboard as a table for pasting into a document the app did not produce, falling back to tab-separated text where a rich clipboard paste is not available. A new investigation starts with whatever rows the edition ships as defaults, if any; existing investigations are never changed.

2Log

Your running sheet, plus the entries the app adds itself when a decision is made.

The running sheet: a timestamped record of investigation activity, displayed as message bubbles grouped by day. Type a line, press Enter, and it is on the record. Entries cannot be edited afterwards (one added in error can be removed), which is what makes the log worth citing. The logbook is included in exports and in the drafting collation as the contemporaneous record.

The application writes to the same sheet as you work. A checklist question answered, a to-do added or ticked, an object created, renamed or removed, a test result recorded, evidence captured or finalised, an interview finalised, a draft landed, an audit run, a report exported, a shared-file conflict resolved: each one appears as its own entry, marked "auto" and shown in a muted bubble. Typing in a text box is never logged, only the change it settles. An automatic entry cannot be edited or removed, which is the point of it, and both kinds go into the drafting collation and the report's investigation activity appendix, so a reader can follow the investigation's own timeline.

#Reading an entry

Choose All, Manual or Automatic at the top of the tab to narrow the list to one kind. An entry that names an object on the map carries a → <object> link that opens it. A manual entry carries a ✕ for removing it; an automatic entry carries neither link nor cross, since there is nothing to remove and nothing of its own to open.

#Adding an entry

The composer sits at the foot of the tab: a box reading Log an action, request, receipt, or decision… (Enter to add), with an Add entry button at the far right for the same thing. Either way the line lands on today's date, in its own bubble, timestamped to the minute. The scope of the investigation (the questions it must answer, and what it will not examine) is worth recording here as an early entry, so there is a dated record of what was decided.

3Evidence

Everything gathered for the investigation, under three sub-tabs: Safety reports, Interviews and Captured material.

#Safety reports

Reports submitted by the people involved: air safety reports, written statements, self-reports. Paste each one in as it was written, with the submitter's role, the date, and the report reference so it stays traceable. Do not tidy the wording; how someone described what happened is itself evidence. These are first-person accounts written close to the event, often before anyone has been interviewed, which makes them some of the best narrative material an investigation has, and they feed report drafting the same way interviews do. They need no writing-up pass of their own: a submitted report is already prose, in the submitter's own words.

Each report is marked as submitted under a confidential or just-culture scheme by default. That flag travels with it into drafting, where the model is told it may use the account to understand what happened and attribute it by role, but must not quote it verbatim or reproduce identifying detail from it. Those protections are the reason people report at all, and a drafted report is a document that gets distributed. Untick it only for a statement given without them.

+ Report adds a new sub-tab, titled by the report once you give it one. A report submitted under a confidential or just-culture scheme carries a small ● lock mark on its sub-tab, so the protected ones are visible at a glance without opening each in turn.

The safety report form

Report details carries the fields that place the report: Title, Submitted by (role), Submitted, and Reference. A Link to the original field and its Open original button sit beside them, for a report that is also filed somewhere else, such as a safety database or a shared drive, so you can get back to the original without hunting for it. The confidentiality tick is below them, as described above.

Report text is the single box the account goes into, pasted in as it was written. Supporting material is the same capture panel an object carries on the map, for anything that came with the report itself. Delete report, at the foot, asks before it removes the report.

#Interviews: the record

One record per interview. + Interview adds a new sub-tab. Double-click a sub-tab to rename it to the interviewee's name once you have it; Delete interview, at the foot, removes the interview after asking.

The record section opens with a badge reading Finalised or Not finalised, so an interview's status is visible without opening it. Below the badge sits Attendees (Lead interviewer, Assisting interviewer, Interviewee and Support person), then the collapsible checklists Preparation and Opening, seeded from the templates in Settings and answered Yes, No or N/A the same way as the investigator's checklist, and Background information for whatever sets the scene before the account itself.

An interview here is a record, and the app asks one question about it first: was it recorded? Recorded means the transcript is the record (see Transcripts and speaker roles). Not recorded means your notes are the record: write them up while the interview is fresh. If it turns out there was a recording after all, importing a transcript switches the interview to Recorded.

#Transcripts and speaker roles

Import transcript… brings in the Teams .docx or .vtt download, or paste it straight in. It is what was actually said rather than what you caught and remembered. Correct it where the transcription is wrong, because Teams mishears names, readbacks and technical words, and a record nobody may fix is worse than one they may. Your own notes sit alongside it as your observations. The transcript box and the note boxes grow with their text to about the height of an A4 page (less in a small window) before they scroll, and can still be dragged taller or shorter.

What you imported is kept exactly as it arrived, under Original transcript above the box you type in. You are correcting a working copy; the source stays where it is, so a correction can always be checked against what the recording actually produced. The card says whether the working copy has moved since the import, and Show original transcript opens the text itself. Nothing edits the original, and only the corrected copy is finalised, exported or read when a report is drafted, so a mishearing you have already fixed cannot come back as evidence. Remove transcript takes both away, since a source with nothing left to compare against is only clutter.

Give every speaker in a transcript a role. Each speaker then appears in the record, the report and anything sent to a model by the role you mapped them to, not by the name the transcript gave them. A speaker you leave unmapped still reaches the record, but as an unidentified speaker rather than by name: dropping their turns would lose evidence, and keeping the name would put it in the report. The mapping covers speaker labels only. A name spoken inside a turn, or typed anywhere else in the record, is kept and sent as written, so write people by role throughout. The mapping is deliberately yours to make: a model that guesses which speaker was the captain can attribute a remark to the wrong person, and nothing downstream would catch it.

#Writing the interview up

You write the interview up in three windows. Free recall is what the interviewee told you in their own words, before you asked anything, in the order they told it. Additional questions is what you asked once the free recall was finished, and what they answered. Follow-up questions is what is still open: what to put to this person again, or to check with someone else. Under them is your own notes box, for what you observed rather than what was said. Where the interview was recorded the transcript is the record and the three windows wait behind Add your own notes, for your own additions to it.

Where the interview was run by another investigator, add their notes with Add notes from another investigator rather than pasting them into your own box, so whose account is whose stays clear in the record and in drafting.

Investigator's notes, at the foot of the record, has its own small toolbar (B, I and • List) for notes written in your own words rather than in the structured windows above, formatted enough to stay readable without being another document to maintain.

#Finalising an interview, and reading it back

Finalise interview when the record says what was said. Report drafting is told the account is settled rather than still moving, and the page stops being a form: the boxes give way to the record itself, read as one document: who was interviewed and when, the background you noted, then the corrected transcript or the three sections in order, then your own notes, with anything you left empty simply not there.

From then on, clicking the interview opens that document straight away rather than the form it was filled in on. A recorded interview carries a long transcript and a page of notes, so a small control above it chooses which to read: Transcript, Notes or Both, which is where it opens. The original transcript stays reachable under the document for comparison. The speaker's name at the start of each turn is shown in bold, here and in the transcript box while it is being edited, so the finished interview is easy to follow. The bold is only how it is shown: the transcript itself is stored as plain text. An interview that was not recorded has only its notes, so no control is offered.

Reopen puts the boxes back, and is the only way to correct a finalised record: the lock is there so an account cannot drift underneath a draft that already quotes it.

#Captured material

Evidence pasted into an object's form, or captured from the timeline or directly, is held once: it shows here too, as a grid of tiles that fills the width of the window, so you see what rests on what without leaving the map. The grid runs newest capture first, within each group, so the file you just dropped in is the one you see.

Each tile carries its caption as its title and the material itself below it: an image as a picture, a text extract as its first few lines, and a file as an icon for its type (PDF, document, spreadsheet, audio, video) with its name. A text capture renders as formatted text rather than raw markdown, so a pasted quote reads as an indented extract. Rest the pointer on a tile, or move to it with Tab, and the rest of the record comes up over the material: category, significance, reference, when it was captured, whether it is finalised, and what rests on it. Each object listed there is a jump: clicking it takes you to the object on the AcciMap, brought into view and flashed. View shows the capture at full size, and Delete removes the item from the register after showing you everything that rests on it. Click a tile, or press Enter on it, to open the item's own form.

Group by, above the grid, chooses how it is split: Nothing (one grid), Where it lives, Category, Lane, or Kind, each group headed with its count. Size beside it sets the tiles at Compact, Standard or Large; the grid fits as many across as the window allows, and Full size cards brings back the original cards with every detail on show. This computer remembers the choice. Evidence attached to an object, a timeline event or a safety report appears as the same small tile in its own form.

Captured material also files by category, shown as sub-tabs across the top, starting with All, each carrying a count of what is filed under it. Only categories something is filed under appear; Show all reveals the rest greyed out, where they work as a prompt: nothing under CCTV yet, on an occurrence that happened in view of a camera. The category list itself is edited in Settings > Evidence > Categories: add, rename, reorder, or remove one, or reset back to the built-in list. An item always keeps its category as its own text, so renaming or removing one there only changes what the picker offers for the next item filed, never material already captured, and typing a category that is not on the list still works: it is kept with that item and shown for it.

#Capturing: paste, drop and size limits

Add to the evidence register is the capture zone itself, sitting above the grid on All; opened from a category sub-tab it reads Add to <category> instead, and anything that lands there is filed under that category from the moment it is captured. Click it and paste a screenshot or a block of text, or drag a file from Explorer and drop it on the zone (PDFs included; the zone lights up as the file comes over it). The capture panel on an object's own form, on a timeline event and on a safety report works the same way. Each capture takes a short note on why it matters.

Everything captured is a copy held inside the .sfm (files to 2 MB, images to 5 MB), so nothing breaks if the originals move. With Settings > Evidence > Attached files on, a dropped file of up to 100 MB is kept in an attachments folder beside the investigation instead. The app is a tool, not a repository: capture the relevant page or passage, not the manual, and keep whole documents in your organisation's investigation folder, which remains the system of record.

#Verbatim, or my description

The application holds no recordings. Audio, CCTV and recorded data arrive as text somebody typed, so a CCTV item is a time and what happened at it, with a screenshot if it helps. That makes one distinction worth stating on every text extract, and each one carries it: Verbatim for the source's own words (a transcript, a quotation from a manual, the text of a message) and My description for your account of what you watched or heard. A report may quote the first. The second is already your interpretation, so a draft will summarise it and never put it in quotation marks or attribute it as anyone's speech. Anything you have not marked is treated as a description, because putting your words in someone else's mouth is the mistake you cannot defend and a reader cannot detect.

#Captures as report figures

An image or a text extract can also go into the report. Its form carries one tick, Include in the report as a figure, off until you set it, and the card then shows a Figure badge on its tile, so you can see at a glance which captures are headed for the document. A dropped file gets no tick, and its form says why: there is nothing in a file that can be put on a page, so capture the page you want as an image instead. A ticked capture is numbered in the order the report argues the objects the captures belong to, so Figure 1 is the first one a reader meets, whichever kind it is, and its caption and reference are what print under it. An image prints inside a thin frame with the caption below; an extract prints as the words themselves in a frame of the same weight, quoted lines set in behind a rule, with the reference as a source line under them. Write the caption as you want it read, because that is the wording that goes into the report. Figures in the report covers where each one lands.

#The evidence item form

The form is four headed sections in one scroll. The first is the capture itself, headed with its type (Image, Text extract, PDF and so on), exactly as it was captured and never editable here, because it is what was captured. An image shows at a readable size with its shape kept; click it, or View full size, to see it whole. A text extract grows with its content up to about half the window and then scrolls, fading at the foot while there is more to read; Read in full opens it in the viewer and Copy text puts it on the clipboard. A file is one row with its type, name and size, and Open writes a copy into the investigation folder's evidence subfolder, beside exports, and opens it in whatever this computer uses for that kind of file. Your own PDF reader beats anything the app could show you.

About this evidence holds Caption and Significance, which start small and grow as you write. Category offers the list set up in Settings and also takes a word that is not on it, which is kept with the item either way. Reference is wherever this item is filed, cited, or numbered outside the app.

In the report holds the tick Include in the report as a figure for an image or a text extract; a file's form says why it has none. On a text extract, choose My description or Verbatim to say whose words these are, and use Summary for the report for the wording a draft will actually use for this extract, with the draft and prompt buttons on the section's heading.

Used by lists everything that rests on the item, each a jump to it. Opened from an object, that object's row carries Unlink from this object, which takes the item off that object without removing it from the register.

Delete item sits at the left of the foot, and asks first, showing everything that rests on the item. At the right, Cancel closes the form without saving, Finalise saves and settles the item, and Save writes your changes and closes it. Ctrl+Enter saves as well. A finalised item's tile carries a Final badge, and its form opens locked, its fields shown as plain text, with Edit and Close at the right. Edit unlocks it in place without asking; Cancel locks it again with nothing changed, and Save writes your changes and reopens the item.

#Files kept beside the investigation

With Settings > Evidence > Attached files on, a dropped file is kept in an attachments folder beside the .sfm rather than inside it, up to 100 MB. Pasted screenshots and text stay inside the file either way. Such a file says so under its name in its tile and its form, with its size, and Open hands it to the system's own reader where it already sits, with no copy. If it is not there (an .sfm passed on without its folder), the tile says "attachment not found" rather than failing when you click Open. An export names the file and says it stays in the investigation's own attachments folder, rather than copying it. Settings: Attached files covers the setting itself.

4Timeline

The sequence of events: when each thing happened, where that time came from, and how firmly it is held.

The investigation's sequence of events: when each thing happened, where that time came from, and how firmly it is held. It is both a working tool and a deliverable. On screen it is a drawn timeline you arrange, with a table under it you edit like a spreadsheet, and the two are views of one record, so a correction in either moves both. At the end it is the sequence-of-events table in the report, and the chronology the drafting model is given.

#Entering a time

Type a time the way you would write it. The app reads 18:05, 1805, 18:05:30, 180530, about 1805 (or ~1805, c. 1805), an optional leading date like 28/08 or 28/08/1998, and a date on its own. What you type also says what you know: typing 18:05 records a time known to the minute, and the report writes it as 18:05 rather than inventing a 00 on the end. Typing 18:05:30 records a recorded stamp, and about 1805 records an approximation, which reads as "about 18:05" wherever it appears. The Known to picker on the event card sets the same thing by hand, and adds Day only for an event you can place on a date but not in a clock. An investigation that has never touched this reads to the second everywhere, exactly as it always did.

#Time bases and corrections to UTC

Sources rarely agree, so the card asks what the recorded time is expressed in: UTC (the default, no correction needed), Local, or Other for a source clock or a device time. Local and Other then ask two things. Which clock names it (AWST, UTC+09:30, ship's clock, FDR clock), and the correction to UTC is the signed adjustment, like -8:00 for a zone or -0:02:14 for a clock error. The card shows the calculated time as you work. The corrected time drives the ordering and every output, while the recorded time and its correction survive into the report, so the trail can be audited: "06:45:30 (recorded 06:47:30 ARFF watch room clock, correction -0:02)". Corrected events are starred on the axis and chipped in the table. The whole investigation can also be worked in local time rather than UTC with the clock toggle, which changes the labelling everywhere including the exports; per-event corrections work either way.

#What an event records

The event itself is the one line that appears on the bubble and in the sequence; detail is what happened more fully, including anything uncertain about the time. Entity is who or what the event belongs to, and gives the bubble its colour. Duration turns a point in time into a span, and an event can carry several, each measured from its start: take-off to aircraft stopped, and take-off to V1. Location is where it happened: a position, a berth, a chainage, a runway.

Two fields say how much weight the entry carries. Kind of source is what sort of source it is (recorded data, voice recording, a traffic service record, surveillance, CCTV, physical evidence, a maintenance or operator record, an interview or account, a document), offered as a list you can type over, and source of information is the specific reference that lets a reader go and check it. They print together: "[Source: Recorded data: FDR channel 12]". Status says how firmly the entry is held: established for something supported by a recorded or physical source, reported for an account that has not been independently confirmed, and estimated for a time you derived yourself. It is blank until you set it, and a blank one claims nothing.

Report narrative is the event in report voice, one or two sentences. The occurrence section of the report is built from these; an event left blank there renders as its plain timeline line instead, and the Record page shows it as a gap to fill.

#The event card

+ Event opens a blank card; Open on a table row or a double-click on a bubble opens that event's own. The card runs in four headed sections, in the order you would fill them in. Event comes first: the Event line itself, then Date (DD/MM/YYYY), Time (UTC), which reads Time as recorded once the basis is anything but UTC, and Known to. A compact row below holds Time is expressed in with its three choices, Which clock and Correction to UTC where the basis is Local or Other, Effective time (UTC), which is worked out from all of that and cannot be typed into. Duration sits under that row as a short list, one entry per span: What it spans (optional, such as Take-off to V1), Length or or end time, which is the other way into the same length, then a colour (Entity colour, the default, or one of six duration colours chosen to stay apart from the entity colours and to read for colour-blind viewers) and where it is drawn, On the axis or Below. The arrows reorder the list and × removes an entry; + Add duration adds another. An event with no duration shows one blank entry, so a first span is just a length typed in. The first entry with a length is the event's main span: it is the end the report's chronology prints, and the one an older copy of the app reads. Location, Phase and Entity close the section, followed by This investigation's own columns where the investigation has added any.

Evidence holds Kind of source and Status together, then Source of information, Detail and Supporting material. Report narrative carries the narrative box, with the prompt actions (Copy AI prompt and Save prompt as file, at the chat tier) on the heading's line and the prompt's size estimate in small type beneath them. The foot stays in view however far the card scrolls, with Delete event on the left, which removes the whole entry, and Done on the right, which closes the card.

#Phases, and the order events are read in

Phase is a free label for the part of the sequence an event belongs to: pre-departure, the transit, the occurrence, the response. Set it on the card or in the table, and the chronology in the report, the exports and the drafting collation breaks into subheadings wherever it changes. Phases already in use are offered as you type, so one phase does not end up with four spellings. A phase that comes round again gets its heading again: the sequence is never reordered to suit the grouping, and an investigation that uses no phases prints with no headings at all.

Order follows the times, always. Correct a time and the sequence re-sorts; drag a bubble and only its layout moves, with the stalk still pointing at the true tick. Where two events share the same corrected time, and only there, the table offers arrows to put them in the order they actually happened.

#Linking to the map and to the evidence

An event can carry supporting material the same way a map object does: paste or drop it in, and it is filed once in the evidence register and linked here. Bubbles with material show a paperclip, and binary captures are written out beside the report when you export.

An event can also be linked to the objects on the map it bears on, under Linked analysis objects, which is you saying that this is the moment that factor acted. Two things follow. The chronology names the factors beside the event, so the sequence and the analysis cannot be read apart, and the record check that looks for a causal arrow pointing backwards in time reads the linked event first, ahead of the weaker readings it otherwise has to fall back on (a shared document, a time written into the evidence text, or where the box sits on the canvas).

#Your own columns, and the Columns dialog

Every organisation keeps one or two things on a timeline that no application can guess: an occurrence reference, a flight phase code, a crew position. Columns… on the toolbar adds them to this investigation. A column has a name and a kind, where text takes anything, number refuses what is not a number, and choice offers a list you can still type over. They appear as columns in the table, as fields on the event card, and in the chronology as "[Occurrence reference: 2026/0412]". They belong to the one investigation, so adding one needs no new version of the app, and deleting one says how many events carry a value before it takes them with it.

The Columns dialog

#Working the timeline

The toolbar carries the controls you reach for most, in two groups. The view group decides how the timeline is shown: a spacing choice of Sequence or Proportional, a working-clock choice of UTC or Local, and the zoom control. The editing group changes the record: Undo (Ctrl+Z) and Redo (Ctrl+Y), + Event, + Entity, Import… and Columns…. Under it sits a quick-add box, reading "Quick add: 14:42:05 Aircraft departs the seal - or 28/08 06:10 Wreckage exam begins (Enter, times UTC)", the inline way to log an event as it is called out rather than opening the card. Below that, the entity legend shows one chip per entity in use; clicking a chip opens its own small editor, with the name, the colour swatches, a count of how many events use it, Delete entity and Done.

Add an event with + Event for the full card, with the quick-add line (21:43:52 Aircraft departs the sealed surface then Enter), or by typing in the table. A new event inherits the last one's phase, since a sequence is usually entered a phase at a time. Create entities with + Entity; each takes a colour, its events' bubbles match, and clicking a legend entry renames, recolours or deletes it (deleting warns how many events use it and leaves those events in place with no entity). Bubbles resize from their corner handle, and every timeline edit is undoable.

The spacing toggle switches between Sequence (even spacing, readable, not to scale) and Proportional (true time gaps). The canvas pans and zooms like the map: drag empty canvas to pan, Ctrl+wheel to zoom at the cursor, wheel to travel along the axis, and Fit to frame everything. Timelines can span days, weeks or months, and day changes are marked on the axis with dated dividers. A duration placed on the axis is drawn as a translucent bar laid over the axis in its colour, starting at its event's anchor dot (its corrected start) and ending with a small tick and its label, such as Take-off to V1, 27 s. Bars that overlap in time sit on thin tracks hugging the axis, alternately just above and just below it, which is how concurrent activity becomes visible, and an approximate start fades in rather than starting sharp. A duration placed below goes on a track in a band beneath the lowest cards, starting at its event's position, with a dotted guide up to the anchor dot and a small dot repeating it, so a second or third span does not crowd the axis; overlapping bars there take a track each. Where a label would run into the next bar or another label, it is shortened to the length alone, or left off. Hover a bar for its name, start, end and length, and its event card lights up with it (hovering a card lights every one of its bars); click a bar to open the event. In Proportional spacing a bar's length is to scale; in Sequence spacing it runs to the events it overlaps, because there the position is the event's order, not its time, and a bar that outlasts every event ends in a chevron. A card with supporting material shows the paperclip count on its own line at the bottom, clear of the title. The exported timeline image draws the same bars.

The zoom control in the toolbar's view group gives the same reach with your hands off the mouse: − and + step the zoom, the reading between them shows where it sits, and Fit returns to the whole timeline. A bubble carrying supporting material still shows its paperclip here, and nothing about reading the canvas changes once you are this close to it. The bar between the drawn timeline and the table resizes the two: drag it, or click it and use the up and down arrow keys, and the height is remembered on this machine.

The table underneath carries the same events as plain rows: Date, Time (UTC), whose heading follows whichever clock the investigation is working in, Phase, Entity, Event and Status, then any columns this investigation has added of its own, then the row's own actions. Move earlier and Move later appear only where two events share a time, for the ordering arrows already described above; Open takes you to that event's card, and a × at the end deletes the row's event outright.

A chronology already held in a spreadsheet comes in through Import…: choose a .csv or .xlsx, confirm what each column holds (the app guesses from the headings, including source kind, status, location and phase), and see exactly what will land, including any row it cannot read, before committing. A cell holding 18:05 imports as a time known to the minute, the same as typing it. Only the rows are taken; the file itself is not kept.

#Where the timeline goes

Into the report as the sequence of events, under phase subheadings where you use them, with the correction trail, the status, the location, the source and your own columns on each line. Into the exports as a PNG of the drawn timeline, and into the report's appendix as that same image. Into the drafting collation as the same chronology, with each event's linked extracts beneath it, so a model writing the occurrence section is working from the record rather than from a summary of it. And into the summary, which takes the occurrence date from the timeline.

#A worked example

A ferry grounding. The investigation works in UTC, and the vessel's clock was local time, ten hours ahead. For the plotter freezing you would enter the recorded time 18:07:06, set the basis to Local, name the clock AEST, enter the correction -10:00, and leave the precision at the second, because it came from the plotter's own event log. Kind of source is Recorded data, the source is "Plotter internal event log, cause code 0x51", the status is established, the phase is "The transit", and the event is linked to the map object for the unreported plotter defect. The deckhand's account that he was sent to collect fares is a different kind of entry: enter it as about 1804, kind of source Interview or account, status reported. The report then reads "08:07:06 [Bridge] Chart plotter display freezes and does not recover (recorded 18:07:06 AEST, correction -10:00) (established)", and the deckhand's line reads "about 08:04 ... (reported)", which is exactly the difference between the two that a reader has to be able to see.

5AcciMap

The map itself: lanes, objects and arrows, and the object form where each object is tested against its evidence.

The AcciMap tab holds the map itself: the lanes, the objects placed in them, the arrows between them, and the form where each object is tested against its evidence. Each control is described where you meet it, together with how the method's tests and classification work inside the app. Building a first map in order is covered in Getting started.

#The canvas and lanes

The six horizontal lanes are organisational levels. The faint italic text inside each lane suggests the kinds of factors that belong there.

  • External Influences: Influences from outside the organisation: regulators, sector standards, oversight and guidance.
  • Organisational Influences: The organisation's own systems: hazard recognition, management of change, risk evaluation, training requirements, organisational communication, assurance activities.
  • Risk Controls: The defences that should prevent or catch the occurrence: procedures, alerts and warning systems, training, operational supervision, work scheduling, facilities and infrastructure.
  • Local Conditions: The conditions on the day: weather, workload, fatigue, distractions, health, knowledge and experience.
  • Individual Actions: What people did: handling, planning, inspecting, communicating, documenting, monitoring.
  • Occurrence Events: The event sequence itself, converging on the critical event.

The heavier line above Local Conditions separates the organisational levels (above) from the individual and situational levels (below). Drag any lane boundary to resize it; see Moving objects and resizing lanes. The canvas is an open workspace: pan anywhere, and the lanes run the full width.

#The toolbar

Three placement tools sit at the left of the toolbar: Select, + Object and + Critical event. Only one is ever active at a time, and the toolbar shows which. Select is the ordinary tool, for clicking and dragging objects already on the map.

#Moving objects and resizing lanes

Move and resize objects directly on the AcciMap. An object’s organisational level is determined by the lane containing its centre when the object is placed, moved or resized. Moving an object into another lane changes its level, which also determines where it appears in the Evidence and Argument Tables.

#The zoom and status panels

#Causal arrows

To connect two objects, hover one, drag from one of the ports on its edge, and drop on the object it led to. Build the chains back from the critical event.

Arrows carry the causal argument, and their appearance reflects its strength. Arrows are claims. If you cannot say in one sentence how the source led to the target, the arrow is not ready to draw.

#Title and risk direction

Title names the object. Hypothesising an event or condition that offers a choice of Increased risk, Decreased risk or Neutral, setting which way the object leans before any test below it is answered. The three are mutually exclusive. Increased-risk objects take sharp corners; decreased-risk objects become green positive events once they exist; a neutral object that exists is recorded as an event or condition with no risk impact.

#The three tests

The tests follow in order: Existence, then Influence, then Importance, each appearing once the test before it is answered.

  • Existence: "Did this event or condition actually exist or occur?"
    • asked of every object, the critical event included
    • a pass turns the border solid; a fail excludes the hypothesis, struck through but kept on the map
  • Influence: "Did it influence this occurrence?"
    • asked once Existence passes
    • a pass makes an increased-risk object a contributing safety factor and turns its outgoing arrows solid green
  • Importance: "Is it important enough to address anyway?"
    • asked only if Influence fails
    • a pass makes an increased-risk object an other risk factor, a finding in its own right

Each test carries a ? beside its heading for guidance on what counts as evidence, a choice of Conclusive or Argument, the text the test rests on, a confidence slider with the probability term it reads as shown beside it, the buttons Untested and Insufficient evidence for where neither answer is ready yet, and a comment field.

The Influence test adds a Reason button beside its box (the single box in Conclusive mode, each of the for and against boxes in Argument mode). It opens a panel of common reasons under six headings: the mechanism, timing and sequence, place and exposure, alternative explanations, consistency with other occasions, and the strength of the evidence. Choose a heading on the left and each reason shows with a line on when it applies, so you can read before choosing; in Argument mode each box offers only its own side's reasons. Choosing one inserts its starting sentence, which you then complete: at the start of an empty box, or on a new line if you have already written something. The arrow keys move through the panel, Enter inserts and Escape closes it. Settings > Analysis > Influence reasons edits the list.

#Classification: what the answers make an object

You never set an object's classification by hand. The app derives it from the risk direction and the test results, every time one changes:

  • IF Existence fails
    • THEN excluded hypothesis
  • ELSE IF Existence is untested or inconclusive
    • THEN it stays a hypothesis
  • ELSE (Existence passes)
    • IF increased risk
      • IF Influence passes: contributing safety factor
      • IF Influence fails and Importance passes: other risk factor
      • IF Influence and Importance both fail: excluded hypothesis
      • otherwise: safety factor, testing in progress
    • IF decreased risk
      • THEN positive event, whatever Influence and Importance say
    • IF neutral
      • THEN event or condition, no risk impact
  • A critical event stays the critical event on the map; its Existence is still established through evidence like any other object's.

Record positive events as carefully as the factors that went wrong: the defences and decisions that limited the outcome deserve reinforcement, and identifying them is part of the investigation's value. The words a report uses for these groups (causal, contributing, other findings, and the safety issue label) come from Settings > Documents > Finding terms. Help > Decision tree draws the same path as a diagram.

6Record

The record assembled into one document in the chosen style: the source every report and draft is written from.

The investigation as recorded: every section assembled straight from what has been entered, in the order and under the headings of the style you choose. It is not a drafted report, which is a different document written on the Draft report tab. Read it here, check it, and export it. Nothing here needs a model: the Record tab is a complete product on its own, and it is also what a whole-report draft on the Draft report tab starts from.

#The record is the source

Everything a report says comes from the record: the objects on the map and their tests, the evidence behind them, the timeline, the interviews and the safety actions. A paragraph or a section of prose is a reading of that record at one moment. When you finalise one, the app keeps a fingerprint of the record it was finalised against, so it can tell you later when the record has moved on and the words have not. A model, where you use one, drafts from the record and is checked against it. It never adds to the record, and nothing it writes reaches a report until you have read it and finalised it.

#What the Record tab shows, and when

The Record tab shows the record and nothing else. It is assembled again every time anything in the investigation changes, so there is nothing to refresh, and it never shows drafted text. Change the style and the page re-titles and re-orders itself into that style's layout. Under the controls row it shows, in order:

  • the line Assembled in the (style) layout, and the sections that layout produces, in order
  • a count of what is outstanding across the whole report: factors without an analysis paragraph, events without a narrative, and group and section narratives not yet written
  • every section of the layout, with each object from the map as a card under its own title and classification
    • IF the object's paragraph is finalised and current
      • THEN the card shows the paragraph
    • ELSE IF it was finalised and has since gone out of date
      • THEN the card still shows it, under a note that it was finalised against an earlier version of the record, followed by the outline block it has fallen behind
      • and an Out of date button under the section opens the object
    • ELSE
      • the card shows the outline block: the evidence, the test results and the causal links a paragraph would argue from
      • and a Not yet written button under the section opens the object at its Analysis panel
  • Record checks down the right, under Errors, Warnings and Notes, each naming the object it is about
  • Also exported at the foot: the Evidence and Argument Tables, one collapsible table per object under its lane

The cards are how this tab renders the document, so it is plain where one object's discussion ends and the next begins; the document itself, and everything exported from it, is unchanged. Export the record as Word, in the controls row, writes the record as it stands, gaps included, so nothing about the state of the investigation is silently dropped. At the No AI tier this is the report: your paragraphs where you have written them and the record's own lines where you have not.

There is no switch here to a drafted version and no check of drafted text. The Draft report tab's finalised sections, assembled in the style's order with a marker on any section not yet drafted, not finalised, or out of date, are read, checked and exported there.

#Analysis paragraphs, and the two kinds of finalising

The unit a report is built from is one object's analysis paragraph: the few sentences that argue what the record shows about that object. It is written on the Analysis panel of the object's form (labelled Outcome and consequences on the critical event). A report's analysis section is these paragraphs, arranged by your analysis groups (or one group per lane until you set any), with a linking paragraph for each group. The findings come from the test results themselves, so a paragraph argues a finding but never decides one.

With AI access off, the paragraph is optional. Where none is written, the record's own lines (the evidence, the test results and the causal links) stand in for it, and the record checks do not ask for one. With Chat or Endpoint on, every object is expected to have one, and the record checks list any that are missing.

An object's form has two separate Finalise controls, and they answer different questions.

  • The Finalised tick at the foot of the form says the object's evidence and tests are settled. The completion dot on the map turns from amber to green, and the Export dialog stops counting the object as unfinished. It says nothing about any wording.
  • The Finalise button on the Analysis panel accepts the paragraph's wording for the report. Only a finalised paragraph reaches the Record tab and the material a drafting pass is given. A paragraph that is written but not finalised is left out as though it had not been written, because nobody has accepted it yet.

Neither one sets the other. The usual order is to settle the evidence and tests, tick Finalised, then write the paragraph and finalise it. Finalising the paragraph first and then recording the test results in the same sitting is fine too: when you save the form, a paragraph finalised while it was open is kept current against the tests you recorded after it.

Once the record behind a finalised paragraph changes later (a test result, a piece of evidence, a causal link), the paragraph is marked Out of date. The badge says so, a note above the text says why with a Re-finalise against the current record button beside it, and the Finalise button reads Finalise against the current record. Read the paragraph against the record on the left, correct it if it needs it, and finalise it again. On the map, the small triangle in the object's bottom-right corner shows where every paragraph stands: green when it is finalised and current, amber when it is written but not finalised or has gone out of date.

Where the chosen style is the executive one, Export slides (.pptx) sits beside it and writes the same content as a slide deck instead. Where it goes depends on the image itself. One with a transparent background (a PNG with see-through pixels, or an SVG) is treated as a logo and sits at the top right of the cover, opposite "Prepared with Synoptic", fitted within about 45 by 16 mm. One with no transparency (a JPEG, or a PNG with every pixel solid) is treated as a photograph and sits below the title, up to the full text width and about 80 mm high. Either way its shape is kept and it is never printed larger than its own size, so a small image stays sharp. Choose image takes a PNG, JPEG or SVG of up to 2 MB, or click the control and paste an image; a small preview shows what is set, and Remove takes it off. An SVG is kept as a picture drawn from it, so every version of Word shows it, and a picture over 1200 pixels on its long side is scaled down so it does not swell the file. It is saved in the investigation file like the Fictional case tick, appears under a Word template as well as the built-in look, and a cover without one simply has no picture on it.

#Record checks

Record checks, down the right of the page, lists everything the record itself can already tell you is wrong or incomplete: a missing outcome, a factor with no path to the critical event, a recommendation with no safety action, an Existence note worded as a measured fact while its own grading sits below virtually certain, an arrow that points backwards in time (read from the times recorded against the objects themselves where there are any, and only from where they sit on the canvas when there are not), prose that has fallen out of date. They are grouped under Errors (n), Warnings (n) and Notes (n). Each entry that names an object is a link: click it to open that object at the point the problem sits.

#Also exported

At the foot of the page, Also exported previews everything that leaves with the record besides the report body. The Evidence and Argument Tables card holds one collapsible table per object, grouped under its lane, with Export Evidence and Argument Tables as Word beside it: the Existence, Influence and Importance results with the text recorded for each, which is the working paper behind every finding. That Word file is a document in its own right: a cover, a contents page listing every lane and every object under it, a page on how to read the tables, the map on a landscape page, then each object's table under its own heading and kept whole on one page wherever it fits. Each table runs down the page one test at a time: a narrow shaded line with the test's name at the left and its result and confidence at the right, such as "Existence" and "Pass, virtually certain (100%)", then the text recorded for it across the full width, with the evidence against where the test was argued both ways. A test with nothing recorded is its result line alone.

The tick List the evidence under each table, in the controls row at the top, adds under each object's table a line per attached item naming where it came from rather than the material itself. Beside the tables card, a second card names the AcciMap and timeline images; for now it is a placeholder, with no preview and no button yet.

#Analysis groups

Groups, beside the style picker, is where the analysis section's cross-factor argument lives: the linking paragraph that says how a set of factors relate to each other and lead towards the critical event, which no single factor's own paragraph can carry. With no groups defined, the analysis falls back to one group per lane, shown in the editor read-only with no linking prose.

Opening Groups shows the Analysis groups panel. Propose by lane and Propose by mechanism each build a set of groups for you to adjust; Clear groups drops them back to one group per lane with no linking prose. Within a group, its title field renames it, Move up and Move down reorder it against the others, and the picker on each factor moves that factor into a different group (a factor left in no group renders last, under "Other safety factors"). Each group has the same Draft, Finalise, Revert and Clear controls as a factor's own paragraph. Done closes the panel and returns you to the record.

Context, and any style section written as prose with no objects behind it, gets the same treatment: click its "Not yet written" gap to open a narrative editor drafted from the section's own guidance and the collation it declares.

7Draft report and AI drafting

Drafting a report from the record at each AI access tier, checking it, and exactly what a model is sent.

The report is built on the Draft report tab, one section at a time, and it lives in the investigation file rather than only in an exported document. The page runs in the order the work does: the Style band at the top, then two zones. Drafting is getting the words: one row per section, in the order they are written rather than the order they print, each showing where it stands. Your report, below it, is the result: the whole report assembled from its sections in printed order, which you read and correct where it stands, with Check and export and the Whole report area beneath it. Pick a style, then work through the rows: draft a section, read it in the report, correct it there, and Finalise it. Drafted text lives only on this tab; the Record tab never shows it.

A model's draft is a first version built from the record, not the report: the investigator works from that head start, reads it against the record, corrects what is wrong or overstated, and finalises it before anything is published under their own name. The audit is an extra check on top of that reading, not a replacement for it. With AI access at No AI there is nothing to draft here, and the tab points to the Record tab, which assembles the record into one document in the style's outline.

#The three AI access tiers: No AI, Chat, Endpoint

It is the same setting for an object's paragraph, a group's linking paragraph, a section and the whole report. The pane says the same under each choice, with what leaves this computer at each. No AI is the tier the app starts at.

Your licence level caps the choice, and the edition can cap it further; the lower of the two applies. A line at the top of the pane says what your licence allows and to when, with Enter a licence or start a trial, or Licence details, beside it. Chat

The app builds the prompt and you carry it to a chat tool your organisation has approved for investigation material, not a personal account, and the reply back. The app makes no network call of its own; you are the transport, and you see exactly what is being sent because you are the one pasting it. The copied prompt asks the model to draft the text, audit that draft against the record it was given (every claim found in the record, every probability word from the scale the prompt sets out, no test notation in the prose), correct it, and return only the corrected text. So there is one paste each way, and no separate audit to copy out.

  1. Open the object's Analysis panel and press Copy prompt. #The Style band

Under the picker is the short version of the chosen style: what it is for, in the style's own words, and a line counting its layout, the number of sections and what they add up to in words, followed by the section names in printed order. Where the app ships a worked example drafted in that style, an Example (PDF) link sits on the same line and opens it in your PDF reader. A style with no example simply has no link.

What this style produces, an expandable line under the picker, open until the first section is drafted, is the longer version: every section of the chosen style with an explanation of what goes in it and which parts of the record it is drafted from. Both it and the section rows are read out of the style itself, so an organisation's own style describes itself here the same way the shipped ones do.

The style decides the sections, their order, and what each one may be drafted from. These are the styles this edition ships with:

  • Standard investigation. The full investigation report, and the longest of these styles: executive summary, event overview, PEEPO analysis, findings summary and appendices. Written for the safety department and the people who have to act on it.
  • Preliminary investigation update. About three pages for executives while the investigation is still running: what is known, what is still being looked at, and what is already being done. Carries no findings.
  • ATSB-style final report. A full-length public report in the ATSB shape: investigation summary, the occurrence, context, safety analysis, findings, then safety issues and the action taken against them. Written for an industry readership. Modelled on published ATSB reports (CC BY 4.0).
  • NTSB-style probable cause report. A full-length public report in the NTSB shape: factual information, analysis, then conclusions carrying the findings and a single probable cause statement with its contributing factors, followed by recommendations. Modelled on published NTSB reports (US government work, public domain).
  • AAIB-style causal and contributory factors report. A full-length public report in the AAIB shape: synopsis, factual information, analysis, then conclusions listing the findings and splitting causal from contributory factors, followed by safety action and recommendations. Modelled on published AAIB reports (Crown copyright, Open Government Licence v3.0).
  • Executive brief. Three to four pages of plain language for the people who decide: what happened, why it happened, what was found, and what is being done. No factual detail beyond what carries the argument.

#The Drafting zone, and an open row

Sections are drafted individually for two reasons. You can redraft one section without rerunning the whole report, which on a local model is the difference between a minute and twenty. And nothing regenerates over the top of your edits: a section you have approved is never rewritten. Instead, each section is given the sections already finalised before it, so it follows on from your approved text rather than repeating it. Working in the style's own order therefore gives the best result.

The Drafting zone's header carries a count, such as "3 of 5 sections done". With AI access at Chat or Endpoint, a row under it carries Length and Advanced, and at the Endpoint tier it also carries Draft whole report, which reads Redraft whole report once a whole-report draft already exists, with Cancel the run beside it while one is under way. Length is a compact choice between no target, Concise, Standard, Extended and a custom word target, with the words and pages of the chosen one shown beneath it; it applies wherever a length target is asked for, and it is remembered on this machine rather than in the investigation.

Each row shows the section's name, a status line and its state as a plain label: Not started, Drafted, Audited, Finalised or Out of date, with Hand-edited beside it once you have changed the drafted wording. A finalised row shows a tick in place of its number. Click a row to open it; only one is open at a time. The open row says in one sentence what the section is for and holds its controls. The row marked Next is the first section still to draft, and it is the one open when you arrive; while it is closed it carries Draft this section, or Start this section where AI access is set to Chat. Once you finalise a section its row closes and the next one opens.

An open row

An open row holds what can be done to that section at your AI access tier. At the Endpoint tier, Draft this section starts it, reading Redraft this section once there is already a draft to replace, with Cancel beside it while it runs. At the Chat tier the same place holds Copy prompt (Copy prompt to redraft once there is a draft) and Save prompt as file, with the prompt's size under them; paste the reply into the box that opens and press Use this text. An argued section such as Safety analysis drafts a few factors at a time (by your approved analysis groups when you have set any, by lane otherwise), shown as Pass 1 of 3 and so on. Finalise accepts the section, reading Finalise against the current record when the record has moved since the draft was made, so finalising is a deliberate choice against what currently stands rather than against what the model saw. Revert to drafted throws your edits away and goes back to the last model draft, and, at the Endpoint tier, Audit this section runs the model audit over this one section. Read it in the report scrolls to the section in Your report. With AI access off, the row says so and points to the Record tab.

Clearing a section

To start a section again, press Clear on its row. A confirmation asks first, then the section goes back to not started, exactly as if it had never been drafted: its text, your edits, its approval and its Hand-edited tag are all gone, and the next Draft starts clean. Clear all, at the head of the Drafting zone, does the same to every drafted section of the style on show and to nothing else (other styles, the whole-report document and the review notes stay as they are). Straight afterwards a line under the Drafting heading offers Undo, which puts back what was cleared, and the Log records the clear and any undo. Undo never overwrites a section you have drafted again since.

#Your report

Your report shows every section in the style's printed order as one document, exactly as Export report as Word writes it, across the full width of the page. A section not yet drafted shows its heading and a marker saying so; one not finalised, or out of date, carries its marker above it with a Finalise button beside it. While no section is drafted for the style it says so in one line instead, with how many object analysis paragraphs are finalised; those are read in the Record tab's assembled view.

Click into any section to edit it where it stands, as the document it is: a small toolbar appears with undo and redo, two heading levels, bold, italic, underline, bulleted and numbered lists and a block quote, and paragraphs work as they do in any word processor. There is no markdown to edit. Each section is its own editing area, so an edit can only ever change the section it is in. Behind the page the section is still stored as markdown, because that is what the model writes, what the next section receives as context, and what the checks, the audit and the exporter read; your edit is written back to it, and merely opening or reading the report changes nothing. An edit marks the section Hand-edited in its row and takes its approval back until you finalise it again, and Revert to drafted, in its row, returns it to the drafted wording. Tables and figures show as they will print but are not edited here. A bracketed marker, such as [NOT ESTABLISHED], is part of the text and must be resolved before a draft goes anywhere.

Every formatting option reaches the Word file as the same formatting: underline prints underlined, a block quote prints as an indented quote with a rule beside it, and the two heading levels print as Word headings. Underline has no plain-markdown form, so it is stored as <u>…</u>; the record check and the audit read the words without the tags. Nothing you format changes what the checks find.

A Findings section whose list contains a finding graded at a band that may be stated with no hedge at all (by default, virtually certain) opens with one sentence saying so: "A finding stated without a likelihood term was established as fact." It is there for the reader, so a plain finding among hedged ones reads as a deliberate statement of fact rather than a missing word, and the Record tab, the Word export and the drafted report all carry it in the same place. It is fixed wording: a drafting pass is told to reproduce it exactly, and the record check and the model audit both leave it alone. Where every finding carries a term, the sentence is left out.

#Check and export

Under the report, Check and export groups its controls by what they do. The Check group holds Check the record, joined by Audit against the record at the Endpoint tier. Repair, offered at that tier once an audit has run, holds Repair against the audit. Polish, also Endpoint only, holds Polish the prose. Export holds Export report as Word. Each one carries a line saying what it does, and whichever is next in the order of work is drawn as the page's primary button, wherever it sits, so the page always points at the same step the rows mark Next.

Check the record is mechanical, not a model opinion: each factor's category and confidence word checked against what its own test result actually says, and, for a Findings section, its categories checked against the derived classification. It also reads the prose itself: more than a couple of semicolons per thousand words is flagged as a sign the text has not been edited into full sentences, and a Findings bullet that negates its subject ("No assessment was likely recorded") is flagged too, because the term then qualifies what did not happen rather than the absence itself; it belongs in front of the whole statement instead ("it is likely that no assessment was recorded"). It runs automatically once an endpoint draft lands (never on a pasted chat reply, which is yours to read before it lands), and the button runs it on demand over everything currently drafted.

Audit against the record, at the Endpoint tier, is the model pass over the assembled sections rather than one of them: it checks the report against the whole investigation, which is the only way to catch two sections that are each defensible on their own and together say different things. The Chat tier has no whole-report audit; its drafting prompts audit themselves instead. What the audit flags is what Repair against the audit acts on, and Polish the prose is the step after it; both are described under Audit, repair and polish.

Export report as Word assembles the sections in the style's order into a Word document and a markdown copy. A section that is missing, unapproved, or out of date is still included, but carries a marker saying so, and the cover page records that the report is incomplete in a Status line under its date of issue. Silently dropping unapproved work, or letting it leave looking finished, would both be worse than a marker you have to delete.

Appendices on the export, beside the export it changes, decides what goes out behind the report itself: an image of the AcciMap, an image of the timeline, and the Evidence and Argument Tables. Each ticked one is appended after the report body under its own heading. The two images each take a landscape page of their own. The ticks are yours rather than the investigation's, so they stay put from one investigation to the next, and they apply to every report export, this tab's and the Record tab's alike, which is why the same ticks appear on both pages. An appendix with nothing to show, a timeline with no events on it, is left out rather than printed empty, and so is one the style already prints itself: where a style's own body carries the Evidence and Argument Tables, that tick is disabled and says "already in this style". Beside it, an Evidence and Argument Tables document tick reading List the evidence under each table adds, under each object's table, a line per attached item naming where it came from rather than the material itself, the same option the Record tab's Also exported card carries.

#Figures in the report

Figures travel with the report. An image you ticked on the Evidence tab goes in after the paragraph that first says "Figure 3", and where the prose never mentions it, at the end of the section that argues the object it belongs to; a capture no object claims closes the document in a Figures appendix. It prints at the text width inside a thin black border, with "Figure 3: your caption" under it. The drafting model is told the figure exists and what it shows, so it can refer to it by number, but whether the argument needs it is left to the draft and to you. In Word the number is a real caption field and every "Figure 3" in the text is a cross reference to it, so Insert Caption, Insert Cross-reference and a table of figures all work on what the app wrote, and adding a figure by hand renumbers the rest. The Record tab's assembled view and Your report show the figures in the same places, so what you read on screen is what the Word file carries.

A drafted section says "Figure 3" and nothing more, and what Figure 3 is gets settled again when the report is exported. So if you tick another image onto a factor the report argues earlier, every number below it shifts and sentences written about one picture now point at another. The record checks say so: a section drafted against a different numbering is reported as out of step, naming what each number now points at, and you redraft the section or correct the numbers yourself. Nothing is silently rewritten, because only you can tell which the sentence meant.

#The whole report

Whole report, at the foot of Your report, holds the report written as one piece. With an endpoint that has the context for the whole investigation, Draft whole report sends the entire record to the model in one run (an outline, then every section against the full collation, a cohesion edit, and an adversarial grounding audit that checks every claim in the draft against the source material), so the report is written as one argument rather than assembled from sections drafted apart, fitted to the chosen length target without dropping established facts. The finished report lands in the Whole report area, and each of its sections also lands in Your report as a draft, so you still read, correct and finalise it one section at a time. A section you have already finalised is never overwritten; the run log, shown in the Whole report area, names any it kept. Any claim the audit marks unsupported or contradicted is the model's invention and must be fixed or cut.

Smooth the whole report instead edits your finalised sections together into one document: shared terms and tense, transitions between sections, repetition removed. It never adds, removes, or reweighs a finding, and it never replaces the section texts above it. Either way the whole-report text is edited in place the same way as the sections, with its own Finalise, Revert to drafted (back to the last pass) and Clear (drop the whole-report document and return to the section-by-section report alone). It goes out of date the moment a finalised section changes underneath it. Export whole report (Export smoothed report for a smoothed one) writes it out as its own Word file, separate from Export report as Word, so it is never confused with the section-by-section report.

#How a section draft is assembled

The Draft report tab writes the report in the chosen style, one section at a time. When a section is drafted, the model is given the assembled record for that section: your finalised paragraphs and narratives, marked to be kept as you wrote them; the outline block for any object that has none; and an out-of-date paragraph as guidance only, never as wording to keep, since nobody has confirmed it still fits the record. That is From the assembled record (default), under Advanced beside the length choice. The other choice there, From the collated evidence only, starts from the collated evidence alone instead, the way drafting worked before the record view existed, which is worth a look only when comparing the two. The model is also given the sections already finalised before this one, so it follows on from your approved text instead of repeating it.

An argued section such as the analysis is drafted a few factors at a time, by your analysis groups or by lane, and the note under the section's buttons says how many passes that is. The more paragraphs you have finalised, the less the model has to write for itself: a section drafted from a fully finalised record is mostly your own words, arranged and joined.

What comes back is a draft. Read it in Your report, correct it there by clicking into the text, and press Finalise. Editing a finalised section withdraws the finalisation, and a finalised section goes out of date when the record changes, the same as a paragraph, with Finalise reading Finalise against the current record until you have read it again.

#Audit, repair and polish

At the Endpoint tier, each factor's Analysis panel has an Audit action: it checks the paragraph against that factor's own record, never the whole investigation, and reports verdicts in the same Checked / Supported / Unsupported / Contradicted / Distorted format as the whole-report audit. It runs on the checking model, set in Settings > AI drafting > Models as a model's Checking model (ideally a different, smaller model from the one that drafted, since a checker from the same family tends to wave through its own confident mistakes). The Chat tier has no separate audit to copy out, because its drafting prompt already carries one. The result is shown inline and is never saved with the investigation: it is a second opinion, checked again by hand, not a certificate.

A paragraph drafted on Endpoint is also swept by a set of mechanical checks the moment it lands: a factor whose Influence test did not pass being written as having contributed, a probability word other than the one recorded against the test, the investigation's own test notation leaking into report prose, and American spelling. These run with no model at all, so they are instant and always available; a Chat paste skips them, on the view that the investigator reads and signs whatever they bring back themselves. The warnings sit under the paragraph and clear the moment you edit the text or finalise it; they never block Finalise.

Repair against the audit

Once there is an audit, Repair against the audit sends back only the sections something was flagged in, with only the flagged claims to correct, re-audits each one, and shows the result beside the text it would replace. A Findings or summary bullet is judged against the narrower seven-term scale those two sections are held to, not the full ten, so a bullet correctly narrowed to one of those terms is no longer flagged as unsupported, and a summary may also state a finding plainly, with no probability term at all, since the Findings section already carries it. Nothing is written until you land it, section by section: the model proposes and you accept. A section the pass could not improve is shown too, with the reason, because "the re-audit was no better" is worth reading.

Polish the prose

Polish the prose comes after that, and Polish this section, in a section's open row beside its Draft and Audit buttons, does one section at a time. It asks for the same meaning in better sentences, and only that: no fact, figure, name, date, probability term or finding may change, nor the order the findings are stated in, and the headings, the figure mentions and the Findings preamble come back word for word. Then it proves it. The polished section has to point at every figure the drafted one pointed at, record the same terms in the same findings, and raise nothing new in the record check. Then a second reading sets the two texts side by side and lists anything the original says and the polished wording no longer says: a fact, a figure, a name, a date, a quantity, a probability term or a qualification. The list has to come back empty. Last, a fresh audit has to leave no more claims unsupported than before, and contradict no more. Anything less and the drafted wording stays, with the reason on screen, naming what went missing. What passes is shown beside the text it would replace and landed by you, section by section.

The Polish group sits in Check and export whenever AI access is at Endpoint, even before anything is drafted: disabled, with "Draft a section first" as its reason, rather than missing outright. A model also has Polish granularity under Advanced in Settings > AI drafting > Models, used only for a server on this machine: set to one paragraph at a time, an experiment for small local models, the pass rewrites a paragraph at a time instead of a section, puts a paragraph that lost something back as drafted and tells you which, and still holds the whole section to every test above before it may land.

Settings > AI drafting > AI access has its own tick under Endpoint, Polish each draft as soon as it lands, off by default and shown only at the Endpoint tier. Turned on, a draft that lands (one section from its row, or the whole report from Draft whole report) goes straight into the same polish pass, over exactly what was just drafted, and is offered the same way the Polish button offers it: nothing lands unseen, and the repair pass is never chained onto this. The Chat tier pastes every reply back by hand, so there is no automatic pass for it to run.

Where a draft came from

Every drafted text carries where it came from and whether it still matches the record. A badge reads "Drafted by" and the model's name, or "Pasted from chat", and a document brought in from elsewhere says that instead. When the record underneath it changes (a test result, a factor's paragraph, the interview it rested on) the text is marked Out of date and its Finalise button reads "Finalise against the current record" until you have read it again. Drafting retries automatically on transient server errors, and the run log in the Whole report area shows each pass of a whole-report draft. Interviews have no drafting pass of their own: the record is yours to write and finalise, and drafting reads it as it stands.

#What is sent, and where it goes

A drafting run sends the whole collated record: every object with its tests, confidences and reasoning, the timeline, the finalised interviews, the evidence, and the safety reports. Interview speakers appear by the role you map them to when the transcript is brought in. Any other name, typed into the record or spoken in a transcript, is sent as written, so write people by role throughout. Where a local model cannot hold all of it, set that endpoint to conclusions only and it is sent the result of each test rather than the whole argument.

It goes to the endpoint you configured and nowhere else. Apart from the trial request described under Licence and levels, which carries no investigation data, the application contacts nothing but the endpoint you configure, and sends no telemetry. If you have configured no endpoint and left AI access at No AI, nothing about the investigation leaves the machine at all.

An API key is kept unencrypted in the app's settings for your Windows account, so anyone who can sign in as you can read it; use a key you can revoke. It is never written into the .sfm, never included in an export or a run log, and removed from any error message the server echoes back, so it cannot travel with an investigation you share or turn up in a screenshot.

Not a consumer chat service

Investigation material is sensitive, and a consumer chat service such as ChatGPT, Claude.ai or Gemini is the wrong place for it. Those accounts sit outside your organisation's data agreements, the content can be retained or reviewed, and you have no way to say afterwards where it went. The application is not designed to be pointed at one: the endpoint route expects an API you or your organisation controls, and the Chat tier expects a chat tool your organisation has approved for material of this sensitivity. #Connecting a model

The investigation itself runs with no model involved. Drafting is the only part of the application that wants one, and how it reaches one is AI access, a single setting you control.

What an endpoint is, in this app's terms

An endpoint is a named profile describing one model server; the Models pane lists them. Each one shows where it sends, worked out from its address (this computer, your network, or the internet with the host named), and when it last passed a test. The one marked In use is the one every Draft goes to. Add a model offers the presets grouped by where the data goes: LM Studio or Ollama on this computer, the same on another computer on your network, and your organisation's gateway or the OpenAI or Anthropic public API, which leave your network.

Each model asks for a Name, an Address, an API key where the service needs one, a Drafting model and a Checking model (picked from the list Refresh list fetches, or typed in), and the Factor detail to send, either the full argument or the conclusions only. The Checking model runs the audit and the checks after repair and polish; a smaller, cheaper model is fine there, and with none set the drafting model runs them. Protocol, Auth header, the Anthropic API version and the experimental polish by paragraph sit under Advanced, since a preset fills them. Done checks the model before it is kept: an address that is missing or still holds a placeholder, a public service with no key, or no drafting model is shown beside its field, and a plain http:// address outside your network is flagged.

Test sends a short test prompt ("Reply with OK.") to the drafting model and, where it differs, the checking model, after asking the server for its model list. It carries no investigation content but costs a few tokens on a paid API, which the button says. It reports on the line beside it whether both models answered, or what failed (a refused key, a model that did not answer), and the result is kept with the time, so the AI access pane can show it.

Protocol is the part worth understanding, because it decides whether a given server will work at all. OpenAI-compatible posts each call to /chat/completions under the base URL, which is the shape LM Studio, Ollama and most corporate gateways expose. Anthropic Messages API posts to /messages instead and sends a version header with every call. Either way the app asks the server's /models path for the model list, and the key, if there is one, goes in the header you name, with Authorization and a Bearer prefix assumed when you leave it blank. A server that speaks neither of those two shapes needs a gateway in front of it that does.

A local model on your own machine

LM Studio or Ollama runs a model on the investigator's own computer, and the app reaches it at an address like http://localhost:1234/v1. The server needs CORS enabled and a capable model loaded with generous context: 32k tokens at minimum, more for a large investigation. On data, this is the strongest option available: nothing leaves the computer, so there is no agreement to negotiate and no provider to trust. On cost, there is none beyond the hardware you already have. On quality, it is the weakest of the three, because a model that fits on a laptop is far smaller than a hosted one, holds less of the record at once and writes less well, which is why the conclusions-only setting exists. There is nothing to ask IT for unless the machine is locked down, in which case you need permission to install the runner.

The same server can run on another machine on the same private network. Reaching LM Studio there needs "Serve on local network" and CORS turned on in its Developer tab; reaching Ollama needs OLLAMA_HOST=0.0.0.0 and OLLAMA_ORIGINS=* set on that machine, and the firewall left open between the two. Once either is reachable, Refresh list (or Test) fetches its model list and shows which model is loaded and at what context, so there is nothing to type from memory. The Models page never contacts a server on its own when it opens.

Your organisation's private cloud endpoint

Most organisations that allow model use at all do it this way: AWS Bedrock, Azure OpenAI, Google Vertex AI, or an internal gateway sitting in front of one of them. On data, the material goes to the cloud but stays inside the organisation's own tenancy and its own agreement with the provider, which ordinarily means no training on your content and a stated retention period. On cost, it is metered per call and paid by the organisation, and a whole-report draft is a handful of large calls rather than thousands of small ones. On quality, it is the best of the three, because these are the frontier models and they can hold the entire record. Ask IT for the base URL, whether it speaks the OpenAI-compatible or Anthropic shape, which header the key goes in, the deployment or model names for the drafting and audit roles, a key, and the retention terms in force so you can record them.

Your organisation's own hosted model

Some organisations host an open-weight model on their own servers and expose it as an internal API. On data, this is as contained as a local model while still being a proper service, since the material never leaves the organisation's own infrastructure. On cost, there is no per-call charge, but the organisation carries the hardware and the people to run it. On quality, it sits between the other two and depends entirely on which model they chose and how much context it is configured to accept. Ask IT the same questions as for a cloud endpoint, and one more: how much context the deployment accepts, because a record that does not fit is the usual reason drafting fails on an internal server.

A hosted public API

An endpoint can also be OpenAI's or Anthropic's public API, or an organisation's own gateway in front of one of them. Settings has an Add button for each, and for an Azure or gateway endpoint, so the protocol and address are filled in for you; all you supply is the key. You create the account and generate the key on the provider's own site and pay them directly for what you use. Whatever such an endpoint drafts or audits, the record it is built from is sent to that provider over the internet, and the Settings pane says so in those words. Point AI access at a hosted endpoint only once your organisation has approved investigation content leaving the machine.

When there is no endpoint at all

Chat is for the common case of an investigator who is allowed to use an approved chat tool but has no API to point the app at. The app builds the same prompt it would have sent, plus the instruction to audit and correct its own draft, you move it across by hand, and the reply comes back into the same provenance and finalising as any other draft. What it does not get is the Endpoint tier's separate audit model and automatic checks, so reading it against the record is your job.

What the published case studies used

The case studies on the website were drafted with a Claude-based pipeline, reached through the suite's own sealed command-line transport rather than an API key, standing in for the kind of private cloud endpoint an organisation would use in practice. The pipeline, the prompts, the audit and the checks are the ones the application ships.

#Analysis: Standard of proof

Required confidence for a finding offers a list of standards in steps of 5 from 50 to 95 per cent, each named by the standard it is and saying which band of the probability scale it opens or sits inside. Different organisations set different standards: transport safety investigators commonly work to likely or probable, which is 70 per cent, the default; the civil balance of probabilities is 50 per cent. A test at or below the mirror of the standard (30 at the default) reads as a fail, and anything between the two is inconclusive; see Confidence, probability terms and the standard of proof.

The standard of proof is kept on this computer, not in the investigation file. A test’s result is worked out when its probability is set, and is saved with the investigation. Changing the standard, or opening the investigation on a computer set to a different one, doesn’t change any result already recorded: the AcciMap, the classifications and the exports keep reading the saved results. Where a saved result no longer matches its probability against this computer’s standard, the object’s form says so beside the test, for example “Recorded as Pass, but 70% reads as Inconclusive against the standard of proof (75%)”, with a button to use the result the current standard gives. Moving the slider records a new result against this computer’s standard. Where several people work on the same investigations, agree one standard and set it on each computer.

#Analysis: Probability wording

Probability wording holds the band edges and the words a report may use for each term on the probability scale. The ten terms themselves are fixed, so findings stay comparable between investigations; what you can change is how each one may be said. "Almost certainly" means the same band as "extremely likely", "probably" the same band as "likely", and a drafting model may reach for any phrase you list. Each row starts at the wording in force, so you edit the list in front of you rather than retyping it, and clearing a row hands it back to the built-in. A phrase already accepted under another term is refused, since a phrase meaning two bands would make every check that reads it ambiguous. The report checks read every phrase in use back as its term, so a report can read like English without a single claim changing strength. The confidence slider on an object's tests stops at 5 and 95 per cent as well as the round tens, so "very unlikely" and "extremely likely" can be recorded exactly rather than only reached by typing.

The pane is a table, one row per band, with three columns: From, Band and Term. The From figure is the lowest percentage the band covers, and you can change it: the app ships with the ATSB's own table from AR-2007-053, and an organisation working to different numbers edits them here. An edge is shared with the band below, so moving one number moves one boundary and the bands can never gap, overlap or swap order; a number outside what its neighbours allow is pulled back to the nearest value that works. The Band column reads the band back to you and names the slider steps that can record it, since two bands at the bottom of the scale, 1 to 4 per cent and under 1, have no slider step at all. Reset every band puts the standard table back. A moved edge follows through everywhere at once: the word the object form shows beside a slider, the standard of proof list, the findings scaffold and every check that reads a term from a percentage.

The Term column holds the phrases, each with a tick that takes it out of use without losing it, so an organisation that does not want "ruled out" read as a probability can switch it off and switch it back on later; a phrase out of use is offered to no drafting pass and read back by no check. The term's own word has no tick box, because a band with no word for itself leaves a check nothing to read. Type in the field itself to add a phrase or take one off the list altogether.

Each term also has a tick reading May be stated plainly, with no hedge for a statement with no hedge at all. It is on for "virtually certain" only, and should stay that way: where the investigation recorded 100 per cent, writing "the pump was unserviceable" says exactly what the record says, while the same flat sentence against a recorded "likely" claims more than the evidence supports. A drafted paragraph that drops the hedge that way is flagged after the draft lands, alongside the check for a term from the wrong band.

#Documents: Finding terms

#Covers, contents pages and the fictional label

A drafted report comes out as a document meant to be read on paper: a cover page, a contents page after it, and every major section starting on a new page. The cover names what the document is, as the report style declares it (a final investigation report, a preliminary investigation report or an executive briefing), with the investigation's name as its title and the date of issue at the foot; it carries no running header or page number of its own. Word builds the contents itself from the headings, which is why it asks once, as the document opens, whether to update the fields in it. Say yes and the entries and page numbers fill in. That question is Word doing its job, not a fault in the file. Word words it as "This document contains fields that may refer to other files", but it is only filling in the contents and the figure numbers, and Yes is the answer. The outline export has no contents page and does not ask.

An investigation marked as a fictional case (the Fictional case tick on the Record tab) says so on every page it exports. #The investigation folder

Start your own with File, New investigation: name it and choose where it lives, and the app creates a folder there holding the investigation file and an exports subfolder that exports and report drafts write into. Opening a captured file writes a copy into an evidence subfolder beside it. The whole investigation (map, checklist, timeline, interviews, evidence captures, logbook) lives in the single .sfm file, unless you choose to keep dropped files beside it (see Files kept beside the investigation), so the folder can sit beside your other material, OneDrive included.

#Saving, autosave and undo

Save with Ctrl+S; a dot beside the project name marks unsaved changes, and autosave writes every five minutes once the file has a home. If a save ever fails the app says so plainly, with a red notice rather than silence. Every edit is undoable with Ctrl+Z, deleted objects are recoverable from the recycle bin, and File, Reopen last restores the previous investigation at the start of the next session.

#Investigations on a shared folder

A .sfm on OneDrive or a network drive can be open on two machines at once, and every save writes the whole file, so the last writer would otherwise win without either person noticing. Two guards sit under that. The app notes the file's size and modification time each time it reads or writes it, and if the copy on disk has changed since, the save is held and you choose what happens: take theirs and reload, save yours alongside as a copy, or write over theirs. There is no merge, because a whole investigation file cannot be merged safely, so the choice is deliberate and it is yours. Beside the file the app also keeps a <name>.sfm.lock while the investigation is open, refreshed about every minute and removed on close, so whoever opens it next is told you have it. The lock only warns: a synced folder can delay it by minutes, and a stale one from a crash or a sleeping laptop is ignored, so the size-and-time check is the guard that actually holds.

11Licence and levels

Free, Professional and Organisation: what each includes, the trial, entering a licence, and what happens when one lapses.

A licence decides a level, and the level decides which features are available. Licensing controls features, never your access to your own investigation data.

#The three levels

  • Free conducts and structures an investigation: the map, evidence, the timeline, the checklist, interviews, the log and the to-dos, and exports of the Evidence and Argument Tables, the map and timeline images, the report-drafting collation and the project snapshot.
  • Professional completes it: AI drafting (an endpoint you set up), Chat AI (copying a prompt out and pasting the reply back), finished Word reports and slides, and your own Word template. The AI is yours to choose; Professional allows every AI access tier you configure.
  • Organisation is Professional adapted to an organisation's own framework, terminology and reporting.

With no licence, the app runs at the Free level, and what it exports goes out clean, with no watermark. A control that needs Professional stays in view, disabled, with the line "Available with Professional." beside it.

#90-day Professional trialNot yet available

Not yet available: the trial is switched on once the licence server is live. A free 90-day Professional trial will be offered the first time the app runs, on one quiet line under the header; Not now puts it away, and Settings > General > Licence offers it again. Give a name and email and the app asks the licence server for a signed trial licence for this email and this installation, once. Only the name, the email, the edition and a random number identifying the installation are sent; no investigation data. Offline, keep working in Free and start the trial later. A build with no licence server configured makes no trial offer anywhere. During the trial the header chip reads "Trial" with the days left.

#Entering a licence

A licence arrives as a block of text beginning IWS1.. Open Settings, choose General > Licence, paste the whole thing into the box (line breaks and all, they do no harm) and press Apply. The pane then shows the level in force, who the licence names, and when it runs out. It is checked on this machine against a key built into the app; nothing is sent anywhere, and it works with no network at all. If a paste is refused, the pane says which of the problems it is: not a licence, not issued for this application, issued for a different edition, or expired. The app header carries a chip reading Free, Professional, Organisation or the trial's days left; hover it for who the licence names and its expiry date, and click it to open this pane.

#When a licence lapses

Nothing is ever locked. When a licence lapses or a trial ends, the app drops to Free and only the Professional actions wait: text drafted while you were licensed stays in the file, readable and editable, and goes out in the project snapshot and the collation as before. Nothing is deleted or hidden, and the app writes nothing to your investigation because the level changed. A stored Word template is kept, unapplied, until a licence allows it again.

A paid licence keeps working for 14 days after its expiry date, so a renewal that arrives late by email does not interrupt an investigation; the pane says when one is in that period. A trial has no such period. One thing to know about the clock: if the machine's date is set earlier than the last date the app saw, the licence is treated as expired until the date is put right.
